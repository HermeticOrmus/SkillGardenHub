Last week I attended the Iterate x CBS AI Club Valentine Hackathon at Columbia Business School.

It was my first hackathon. It won't be my last.

Five strangers. One problem: credentials don't mean what they claim to mean.

LinkedIn says I "know" things. But knowing and proving are different verbs. Most platforms measure consumption -- hours watched, courses completed, streaks maintained. None of that tells you whether I can actually think in the subject.

That's the problem I wanted to solve. Not abstractly. For myself.

We built SkillGarden -- an AI-powered learning platform where content gets validated before it enters your knowledge, skill trees enforce prerequisites (can't cut yew before willow), and mastery follows an exponential curve inspired by RuneScape's leveling system. Level 92 is halfway to 99. Early progress is fast. True depth costs exponentially more effort. That's what makes a Level 99 credential mean something.

My role was architecture and technical lead. But the contribution I'm most proud of was the UX argument I kept making:

Does this feel like a mentor who respects your intelligence, or a mobile game trying to keep you hooked?

We killed the streak counter. Removed urgency indicators. Replaced "Reviews Due" with "Knowledge Gaps Detected." Made credential cards show how mastery was verified -- Socratic debates, voice assessment, claim validation -- not just a badge with a number.

The cross-domain background I carry turned out to be exactly the right toolkit for this problem. Design sensibility shaped how the credential card communicates trust. Business operations informed the prerequisite-gating architecture. Years of learning across unrelated disciplines gave me strong opinions about what real knowledge retention looks like.

I didn't win anything. I built something I want to use. And I discovered that hackathons are exactly my kind of environment -- compressed time, real problems, strangers becoming collaborators in hours. I'm hooked.

The team brought five different domains to one platform -- and that's the point. SkillGarden doesn't care what you study. It cares whether you actually know it.
